Introduction to Pharmacy for Technicians

Welcome to your essential resource for mastering the core knowledge required for a successful career as a Pharmacy Technician. This hub provides clear, accessible information on the topics tested on the certification exam — from medications and calculations to laws, patient safety, and supply-chain rules.

A Pharmacy Technician works under the supervision of a licensed pharmacist to ensure patients receive the correct medications safely and efficiently. Duties range from preparing prescriptions and managing inventory to processing insurance and supporting patient care.

Updated for the 2026 PTCE. This hub and its companion Pharmacy Technician Training Workbook are aligned to the PTCB content outline effective January 6, 2026. See the 2026 Blueprint for how the exam is weighted.

The 2026 PTCB Exam Blueprint

The Pharmacy Technician Certification Exam (PTCE) has 90 multiple-choice questions (80 scored, 10 unscored) taken in two hours. It covers four knowledge domains, weighted as shown. Study every domain, then give extra time to your two weakest areas.

Knowledge DomainWeightWhat It Covers
1. Medications35%Generic/brand names & classes, interactions, therapeutic duplication, doses, dosage forms, side effects, storage & stability.
2. Patient Safety & Quality Assurance23.75%Error prevention, high-alert & look-alike/sound-alike medications, error reporting.
3. Order Entry & Processing22.5%Pharmacy calculations, prescription processing, equipment, and inventory.
4. Federal Requirements18.75%Controlled substances, DEA forms, FDA recalls, DSCSA, and record-keeping.

What changed in 2026

  • Federal Requirements grew (12.5% → 18.75%) — more law and supply-chain content.
  • The Drug Supply Chain Security Act (DSCSA) is a new required topic.
  • Compounding (sterile and non-sterile) and alligation calculations were removed.
  • Narrow Therapeutic Index (NTI) as a standalone topic was removed.
  • New question formats appear: “select all that apply” and image hot-spot items.

Pharmacology Basics

Pharmacokinetics: ADME

Pharmacokinetics describes how the body affects a drug through four processes:

  • Absorption: the drug moves from its administration site into the bloodstream.
    Example: An oral tablet must dissolve and be absorbed; an IV injection bypasses the GI tract and acts almost immediately.
  • Distribution: the drug spreads from the blood to tissues and organs.
    Example: A highly protein-bound drug like warfarin has less free drug available to act.
  • Metabolism: the body chemically alters the drug, mainly in the liver (CYP450 enzymes).
    Example: Two drugs using the same liver enzyme can raise each other's levels and side effects.
  • Excretion: the drug and its metabolites leave the body, mainly via the kidneys.
    Example: Patients with kidney impairment may need lower doses of renally excreted drugs.

Dosage Forms & Routes of Administration

Common Dosage Forms

  • Tablet: solid compressed powder; swallowed, chewed, or dissolved.
  • Capsule: medication in a soluble gelatin shell.
  • Solution: drug fully dissolved in a solvent; appears clear.
  • Suspension: undissolved particles in liquid; shake before use.
  • Cream / Ointment: semi-solid topical preparations (ointment is greasier).
  • Patch (Transdermal): adhesive patch delivering drug through the skin.
  • Suppository: solid form inserted into a body orifice; melts to release drug.
  • Inhaler (MDI/DPI): delivers medication directly to the lungs.
  • Injection: liquid given by needle (IV, IM, or SQ).

Routes of Administration

  • Oral (PO): by mouth; most common.
  • Topical / Transdermal: on the skin for local or systemic effect.
  • Parenteral: by injection — IV (vein, fastest), IM (muscle), SQ (under skin), ID (dermis).
  • Inhalation: into the lungs.
  • Rectal, Vaginal, Ophthalmic (eye), Otic (ear), Nasal: site-specific routes.

Pharmacy Calculations

2026 note: Alligation and compounding were removed from the current exam blueprint. They remain useful pharmacy math and are kept below as optional background. Full worked practice for every type is in the companion workbook.

1. Days' Supply

  • Problem: Amoxicillin 250 mg capsules, 1 capsule three times a day (TID) for 14 days. How many capsules to dispense?
    3 doses/day × 14 days = 42 capsules.
  • Problem: Insulin Glargine 100 units/mL, inject 20 units daily, 10 mL vial. Days' supply?
    10 mL × 100 units/mL = 1,000 units; 1,000 ÷ 20 = 50 days.

2. Clark's Rule (pediatric, by weight)

  • Formula: (weight in lb ÷ 150) × adult dose. Problem: 75-lb child, adult dose 500 mg.
    (75 ÷ 150) × 500 = 250 mg.

3. Young's Rule (pediatric, by age)

  • Formula: (age ÷ (age + 12)) × adult dose. Problem: 6-year-old, adult dose 10 mg.
    (6 ÷ 18) × 10 = 3.33 mg.

4. IV Flow Rate

  • Formula: Volume ÷ time. Problem: 1,000 mL over 8 hours.
    1,000 ÷ 8 = 125 mL/hr.

5. Dilution (C1V1 = C2V2)

  • Problem: How much 20% stock makes 250 mL of a 5% solution?
    V1 = (5 × 250) ÷ 20 = 62.5 mL of stock.

6. Business Math

  • Problem: Drug costs $15.50; patient pays $10 copay, insurance pays $8.25. Profit?
    $18.25 received − $15.50 cost = $2.75.

Alligation removed from 2026 exam

  • Optional: Prepare 60 mL of 50% alcohol from 90% and 20% solutions.
    Parts: 90% = 50−20 = 30; 20% = 90−50 = 40; total 70. 90%: (30/70)×60 = 25.7 mL; 20%: (40/70)×60 = 34.3 mL.

Terminology & Abbreviations

Basic Terms

  • Pharmacology: the study of drugs and their effects on living systems.
  • Pharmacokinetics: how a drug moves through the body (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion).
  • Adverse Reaction: an undesired or unexpected effect of a medication.
  • Analgesic: relieves pain (e.g., ibuprofen).
  • Antipyretic: reduces fever (e.g., acetaminophen).
  • Contraindication: a situation where a drug should not be used because it may be harmful.
  • Deductible: amount an insured person pays before insurance begins to pay.
  • Co-payment: a fixed amount paid for a covered service or prescription.

Laws & Regulations

  • HIPAA (1996)

    Protects the privacy and security of patient health information (PHI). Technicians must safeguard confidentiality and handle PHI appropriately.

  • Controlled Substances Act (DEA)

    The DEA enforces the CSA, regulating controlled substances via five schedules (C-I to C-V) and strict rules for ordering, storage, inventory, and record-keeping.

  • FD&C Act (1938)

    Gives the FDA authority over drug safety. The Durham-Humphrey Amendment (1951) set the Rx vs. OTC distinction; the Kefauver-Harris Amendment (1962) required proof of efficacy.

  • Poison Prevention Packaging Act (1970)

    Requires child-resistant packaging for most prescription drugs unless the patient requests otherwise or the drug is exempt.

  • OBRA '90

    Requires pharmacists to offer to counsel Medicaid patients about their prescriptions.

  • State Boards of Pharmacy

    Each state's board regulates licensure, CE, technician ratios, and scope of practice. Follow the stricter of state or federal rules.

DEA Number Verification

A DEA number is 2 letters + 7 digits. To check the last digit: add the 1st, 3rd, and 5th digits (Sum A); add the 2nd, 4th, and 6th digits and multiply by 2 (Sum B); the last digit of (Sum A + Sum B) should equal the 7th digit.

Example: AB1234563 → (1+3+5)=9; (2+4+6)=12×2=24; 9+24=33; last digit 3 = check digit → valid.

FDA Recall Classes

  • Class I: reasonable probability of serious harm or death.
  • Class II: may cause temporary or reversible health problems.
  • Class III: unlikely to cause harm but violates FDA regulations.

Drug Supply Chain Security Act (DSCSA) — new for 2026

The DSCSA creates an electronic, package-level system to trace prescription drugs through the U.S. supply chain and keep counterfeit or illegitimate products out.

  • The three T's: Transaction Information, Transaction History, and Transaction Statement.
  • Transaction records must be retained for 6 years.
  • Products carry a serialized product identifier (2D data-matrix barcode with GTIN, serial number, lot number, and expiration date).
  • Only authorized (licensed/registered) trading partners may buy or sell.
  • Suspect or illegitimate products are reported to the FDA (not the DEA).
  • Product identifiers must be verified upon request.

Patient Safety & Quality Assurance 23.75%

The second-largest domain focuses on preventing, catching, and reporting medication errors.

High-Alert Medications

These carry a heightened risk of serious harm if used in error:

  • Insulin
  • Anticoagulants (heparin, warfarin)
  • Opioids (fentanyl, morphine)
  • Concentrated electrolytes (potassium chloride)
  • Chemotherapy agents
  • Neuromuscular blockers

Safe Practices to Know

  • Leading zero: write 0.5 mg, never .5 mg (risk of “5 mg”).
  • No trailing zero: write 5 mg, never 5.0 mg (risk of “50 mg”).
  • Avoid error-prone abbreviations (U for units, QD, QOD) — the ISMP “Do Not Use” list.
  • Verify with the NDC and barcode scanning; use two patient identifiers.
  • The five rights: right patient, drug, dose, route, and time.
  • Report errors to ISMP (MERP) or the FDA's MedWatch program; reporting is non-punitive.

Technician Roles & Responsibilities

  • Dispensing: counting, pouring, measuring, and labeling medications.
  • Inventory management: ordering, receiving, stocking, and handling recalls/expirations.
  • Patient information: updating demographics, insurance, and medication histories.
  • Insurance processing: claims, rejections, co-pays, and deductibles.
  • Communication: phones, providers, and patients (under pharmacist supervision).
  • Automated systems: loading and maintaining dispensing cabinets (Pyxis, Omnicell).
  • Records & compliance: dispensing logs, controlled-substance logs, and HIPAA privacy.
Pharmacist-only tasks: final clinical verification, patient counseling on new medications, and clinical judgment calls remain the pharmacist's responsibility.
